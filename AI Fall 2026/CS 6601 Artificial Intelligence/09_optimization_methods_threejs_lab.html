One landscape. Three ways to search it.

Hill climbing, simulated annealing, and genetic algorithms are not three unrelated topics. They are three policies for deciding where to look next when checking every possible answer is too expensive.

Important distinctionThis lab solves one clinic-location problem. It is optimization, not model training, and nothing reusable is learned after Reset.

Practical translation: where should a new clinic go?

The terrain is a teaching model of city demand. Several neighborhoods create attractive peaks, but only one location has the best overall coverage.

Candidate / stateOne proposed clinic coordinate, such as (2.1 east, 1.4 north)

NeighborA nearby site produced by nudging the current coordinates
Highest peakThe best site in this simplified search space
Algorithm outputA location—not a trained prediction model

Why teach all three together?

They expose the central optimization tradeoff: exploit what already looks promising, or explore alternatives that might lead somewhere better.

Hill climbingFast and simple, but a smaller local peak can trap it.
AnnealingTemporary bad moves can cross valleys; cooling gradually restores greediness.
Genetic algorithmA population samples several regions and creates variation through crossover and mutation.
A visual analogy, not a claim that real clinic planning has only two variables. Real deployments require equity, capacity, policy, geographic, and community constraints.
