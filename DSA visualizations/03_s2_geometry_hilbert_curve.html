Google S2 Geometry · indexing on the sphere

Every place on Earth, numbered along one line

S2 is Google's library for geometry on the sphere. It wraps the Earth in a cube, splits each cube face into a quadtree of cells, and threads a Hilbert curve through every cell. Any spot on the planet becomes one 64-bit integer. Spots that are close on the ground usually get integers that are close on the line.

The problem

A latitude–longitude grid is uneven and breaks at the edges

A database sorts numbers quickly, but a location is two numbers on a curved surface. Cutting the globe into 1° × 1° boxes gives boxes of about 12,400 km² at the equator and about 2,300 km² between 79° and 80° N, so one box means very different things in Quito and in Svalbard. The boxes pinch into slivers at the poles, and the grid tears at the 180° meridian, where longitude jumps from +180 to −180.

S2 replaces that grid with cells that stay within a factor of about 2 in area everywhere on Earth, and it gives every cell, at every size, a single integer key. The six steps below follow one coordinate all the way to that key.

Step 1 of 6 · Cube

Project the sphere onto a cube

Write the location as a unit vector (x, y, z). The coordinate with the largest absolute value, together with its sign, picks one of the six cube faces. Dividing the other two coordinates by it gives (u, v), each between −1 and 1. For face 0 that is u = y/x, v = z/x.

This is a gnomonic projection. Each point slides outward along the straight line from Earth's center until it meets the cube. A plane through the center cuts the sphere in a great circle and cuts a cube face in a straight line, so straight lines on a face are great circles on the globe. Every S2 cell edge is therefore a geodesic, the shortest path between its two corners.

Face 0 is centered on 0°, 0° in the Gulf of Guinea. Face 1 is centered on 0°, 90° E in the Indian Ocean, and face 2 on the North Pole. Face 3 sits on 0°, 180° in the central Pacific, face 4 on 0°, 90° W near the Galápagos, and face 5 on the South Pole.

Step 2 of 6 · Warp

Even out the cell sizes before splitting

Equal steps along a cube face are not equal steps on the sphere. Near the middle of a face a step in u sweeps a wide arc of the globe; near the edge the same step sweeps a narrow one. Splitting u evenly leaves the cells at a face's center about 5.2 times larger in area than the cells in its corners.

S2 bends the coordinate first. It maps u to s = ½·√(1 + 3u) for u ≥ 0, mirrored for negative u, so s runs from 0 to 1 across the face. Equal steps in s give cells whose areas differ by at most about 2.1 times. The tangent version, which uses an arctangent so that each axis is cut at equal angles, gets to 1.4 times. S2's own benchmark puts it at roughly three times the cost, so the library uses the quadratic version.

Step 3 of 6 · Quadtree

Split each face into a quadtree

Each face is one level-0 cell. Splitting every cell into 2 × 2 children gives the next level, and S2 goes down to level 30. A cell at level k has exactly one parent at level k − 1, so the levels nest without gaps or overlaps. On a face, the position after the warp is a pair of 30-bit integers (i, j), and the cell at level k is just the top k bits of each.

Level 30 holds 6 × 430, about 6.9 × 1018 leaf cells, averaging 0.74 cm² each. The averages below use Earth's mean radius of 6,371.01 km, the value in S2's S2Earth.

Step 4 of 6 · Hilbert curve

Put the cells in order with a Hilbert curve

The quadtree says which cells exist. It does not say how to line them up, and a database index is a line. S2 orders cells along a Hilbert curve. Inside any cell, the curve visits the four children in a ∩ shape. It then repeats that shape inside each child, rotating or mirroring each copy so the exit of one child sits next to the entrance of the next.

Every level adds one base-4 digit: the child's place in that visit order. Read from the top level down, the digits are the cell's position on the curve. S2 keeps this as four orientations and two small lookup tables, kIJtoPos and kPosToIJ, which this page uses directly.

The four orientations. A third table, kPosToOrientation, sets each child's orientation: the child at position 0 swaps its axes, the child at position 3 swaps and inverts, and children 1 and 2 keep the parent's orientation. That one rule produces every turn in the curve.

The point of the curve is locality. Consecutive positions are always side-by-side cells, so any run of consecutive positions forms one connected patch. Z-order (Morton order), the simpler alternative, interleaves the bits of i and j. It jumps across the square at each quadrant boundary, and a run of its positions can split into separate pieces. Slide the highlighted run below to compare.

The guarantee runs one way only. Cells that are close on the curve are close on the ground, but two cells that touch across a big quadrant boundary can be far apart on the curve. At order 3, the two cells that touch across the vertical center line at the bottom edge are positions 5 and 58. That is why a covering usually needs a few ID ranges rather than one.

S2 joins the six faces into one loop. Odd-numbered faces start with their axes swapped, which keeps every face's (u, v) frame right-handed and lands the last cell of each face next to the first cell of the following face.

Step 5 of 6 · Cell ID

Pack face, path and level into 64 bits

An S2 cell ID is one unsigned 64-bit integer. The top 3 bits hold the face, 0 to 5. Each level below that adds 2 bits, its Hilbert digit. After the last digit comes a single 1 bit, and zeros fill the rest. The position of that trailing 1 tells you the level, so one integer carries the face, the path and the size of the cell.

This layout turns spatial questions into integer arithmetic. Every descendant of a cell has an ID between that cell's range_min and range_max, so "is this point inside this cell?" is two comparisons. Clearing the low bits and setting a new trailing 1 gives an ancestor. The token is the hex form with the trailing zeros removed, so a coarse cell has a short token and a leaf has a 16-character one.

Pick a place and drag the level slider. The view zooms down the quadtree and keeps the chosen cell framed inside the cell three levels above it.

Step 6 of 6 · Covering

Cover a region with a handful of cells

To index or search a shape, S2's S2RegionCoverer approximates it with a small set of cells of mixed sizes: big cells inside, small cells along the boundary. The max_cells budget trades tightness for count. Because nearby cells have nearby IDs, the cells merge into even fewer contiguous ID ranges, and each range is one scan of a sorted index.

A query such as "everything within 2 km of here" becomes: cover the circle, scan the stored cell IDs that fall inside those ranges, then run an exact distance test on what comes back. The covering can return extra candidates near the edge, but it never misses a point inside the region. CockroachDB documents its spatial index in exactly those terms: false positives, no false negatives.

The demo is a simplified model. It uses one flat square patch, tests the circle in the plane, and runs the coverer's greedy loop: expand the largest candidates first while the budget allows, then merge any four siblings into their parent. The San Francisco example below runs the full algorithm on real S2 cells, including the starting cells from the cap's bound and the final normalization. There it returned the same cells as the Rust s2 crate on 50 random circles and budgets.

Worked example · Downtown San Francisco

A radius search over real streets

The map is an OpenStreetMap extract of downtown San Francisco and SoMa. Each of its 1,398 street intersections is stored by its level-30 cell ID in one sorted array, the way a database index would hold it.

Click or tap the map to search around a spot, or focus it and use the arrow keys. The page covers the circle with real S2 cells, turns each cell into its range_min to range_max interval, merges touching intervals, and binary-searches the sorted array once per interval. Only the intersections inside those intervals are read. An exact distance test then drops the ones that sit in a covering cell but outside the circle.

The grid shows every S2 cell of one level. A level-13 cell is about 1 km across here; a level-17 cell is about one city block. Turn on the curve to see the Hilbert order those cells follow across the city.

The covering loop, its starting cells and its final normalization are ported from the libraries' own RegionCoverer. Only the test of a cell against the circle is approximated, in a flat frame around the center, which is accurate at city scale. Run against the Rust s2 crate on 50 random circles, with radii from 32 to 797 m and max_cells budgets from 3 to 35, it returned the same cells every time. The default search is the 400 m circle around the Ferry Building that the code samples below use, so the token lists can be compared directly.

Adoption

      
Why so many systems use it

    

    

      

        
One grid for the whole planet

        
The cells are defined on the sphere, not on a flat map, so there are no zones to pick, no seam at the 180° meridian and no blow-up at the poles. The same code works in Lagos, Helsinki and McMurdo Station, which matters for any product with users on every continent.

      

      

        
A location becomes a sortable integer

        
Any store that keeps integers in order (a B-tree, a key-value store, a range-partitioned distributed database) becomes a spatial index without new data structures. CockroachDB chose S2 because the R-tree indexes that PostGIS relies on did not fit the way it splits data across machines.

      

      

        
The hierarchy lives in the bits

        
An ancestor is the same integer with its low bits cleared and a new trailing 1 set, so rolling data up from street level to city level is integer masking. Containment is two comparisons. Coarse and fine data can share one index.

      

      

        
Even cells, cheap math, open code

        
Cell areas stay within about 2.1 times of each other, edges are geodesics, and the point-to-cell path needs no trigonometry after the unit vector. Google released an early version in 2011 and the maintained C++ library in 2017 under Apache 2.0, with ports to Go, Java and Python. BigQuery exposes it as S2_CELLIDFROMPOINT.

      

    


    

      
Compared with H3

      
Uber's H3 grid uses hexagons, which give every cell six neighbors at the same distance. That suits movement analysis and smoothing over neighbors. Hexagons cannot be split exactly into smaller hexagons, though, so an H3 parent only approximately contains its children. S2's squares nest exactly, which lets one ID range stand for exactly one region. That property is why S2 fits range-scanned database indexes so well.

    


    

      
A common question

      
Is S2 especially useful for Europe?

      

        
No. S2 is deliberately region-neutral, and Europe gets no special treatment. Europe even straddles two faces: by this page's own math, Madrid, Rome and Athens fall on face 0 while London, Paris, Berlin and Moscow fall on face 2. The seam runs along 45° N at the Greenwich meridian and dips to about 41° N at Istanbul's longitude. Cell IDs on either side of a face seam are far apart numerically, so a covering that crosses it needs separate ranges.

        
Inside Europe there are official regional grids built for exactly this kind of work. The INSPIRE and EEA reference grids sit on the ETRS89 Lambert equal-area projection (EPSG:3035), and Eurostat's GEOSTAT population grid uses it at 1 km. For statistics that stay inside Europe, an equal-area regional grid gives exactly equal cells, which S2 cannot. S2 earns its place when data crosses continents, when the key must be one integer in a database, or when one hierarchy has to serve from 1 cm to a whole face.

        
The cells most distorted in shape sit at the eight cube corners, where three faces meet and each corner angle opens to 120°. (The smallest cells by area are elsewhere, at the midpoints of the face edges.) The corners fall at latitude ±35.26° and longitude ±45° or ±135°. Lineage

      
From a curve that fills a square to a planet index

    

    
The name comes from mathematics: S² is the 2-sphere, the surface of a ball in three dimensions. The library's main author is Eric Veach, who was known in computer graphics long before S2. With Leonidas Guibas he introduced multiple importance sampling in 1995, and his 1997 Stanford thesis formalized Monte Carlo path tracing. The Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences recognized that work in 2014.

    
The curve is older still. When Giuseppe Peano showed in 1890 that a continuous curve can pass through every point of a square, the result looked like a paradox. David Hilbert published a simpler construction the next year, built by repeatedly splitting a square into four and joining the quarters. That recursive split is the same move S2 makes on each cube face.
