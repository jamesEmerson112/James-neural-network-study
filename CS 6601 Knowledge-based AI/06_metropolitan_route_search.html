01 / THE FAMILY

One shortest path, five ways to avoid looking everywhere

“Scanning” is not a sixth competitor. It is the shared inner operation: settle the cheapest unsettled intersection, then relax—try to improve—the cost labels of its outgoing neighbors.

Two waves

Bidirectional Dijkstra

priority = g

Search forward from the start and backward from the destination. Stop once no undiscovered route can beat the best meeting route.

Aim

ALT

priority = g + hALT

A* uses landmark distances plus the triangle inequality. A landmark is a measuring post, not somewhere the route must visit.

Hierarchy

Reach

prune when r(v) is too small

A local intersection should not appear in the middle of a long cross-city shortest path. Reach turns that intuition into a safe pruning rule.

Compress

Reach + Shortcuts

u → · · · → v becomes u ⇢ v

A shortcut stores the exact cost and unpacking path of a road chain, letting a query cross an unimportant region in one relaxation.

Combine

Reach + Short + ALT

aim + prune + jump

Landmarks say which direction matters; reach says which roads do not; shortcuts skip the roads removed from detailed consideration.

Where is ordinary Dijkstra? It appears in the guided lab as the one-direction foundation. The historical table uses its faster bidirectional form.
Where is ordinary A*? It is another teaching step. ALT is A* supplied with a stronger, precomputed heuristic than straight-line distance.
g(v) = cheapest known cost from S to vh(v) = proven lower bound from v to Tf(v)=g(v)+h(v) = A* priorityr(v) = reach label

05 / REACH MICROSCOPE

How “important” is an intersection to long shortest paths?

Reach is not traffic volume, popularity, or geometric centrality. It is defined only by where a vertex can sit along shortest paths.

Selected intersection

Move the selector or click the heat map. Cool local intersections have low reach; warm bridge, arterial, and freeway vertices tend to survive longer-route searches.

Reach label (minutes)—
Safe pruning idea
r(v) < min(g(v), lowerBound(v,T))

If the journey has already traveled farther than this vertex’s reach on both sides, that vertex cannot occupy the middle of an optimal S→T route.

r(v) = max over shortest paths P containing v
min(distance to P's start, distance to P's end)

Worst case

These exact algorithms may still inspect most of a graph. A stronger index changes typical work dramatically without magically deleting the worst case.

Space

Dijkstra keeps per-query labels. ALT adds roughly O(kV) landmark distances. Reach adds labels; shortcuts add arcs plus unpacking data.

The dash and 0.5 GB

“—” says no preprocessing time. The baseline still occupies memory because the road graph itself must be stored.

Why was plain Reach “impractical”? Its preprocessing did not scale acceptably on this particular 30M-vertex experiment. Adding shortcuts made reach preprocessing practical and queries far smaller; it did not rescue an inexact answer.

07 / LORE

Each generation taught the search something new

The mid-2000s problem was exact driving directions on continent-scale graphs, often with many queries over the same mostly static road network.

1959

Dijkstra

Let exact distance radiate outward. The next settled label is final when edge costs are nonnegative.

1968

A*

Add an admissible estimate of what remains, directing the wave toward a goal without losing optimality.

2004

Reach

Ron Gutman formalized a road-hierarchy signal from shortest paths and used it for safe pruning.

2005

ALT

Andrew Goldberg and Chris Harrelson turned landmark distances and triangle inequalities into powerful A* bounds.

2006

Reach for A*

Goldberg, Haim Kaplan, and Renato Werneck combined bidirectional reach, shortcut arcs, and A* guidance.

Primary reading: Goldberg & Harrelson, “Computing the Shortest Path: A* Search Meets Graph Theory” (2005) · Goldberg, Kaplan & Werneck, “Reach for A*” (2006). Historical benchmark values above are transcribed from the supplied lecture slide.
