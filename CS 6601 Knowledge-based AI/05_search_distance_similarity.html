Knowledge-based AI · Search foundations

Search needs a strategy—and sometimes a compass.

A* is a way to explore possibilities. Euclidean distance, Manhattan distance, dot product, and cosine are ways to score relationships. They are connected, but they are not interchangeable.

The word that causes the confusion

AI uses “search” for both finding a path through states and retrieving a nearby vector. Only the first one necessarily has a route.

01 / FIRST, SEPARATE THE JOBS

A score does not perform the search.

The search strategy decides what to examine next. The score supplies information the strategy may use. A* combines the cost already paid with an estimate of what remains.

INPUT

Problem representation

Locations on a map, configurations of a cube, or vectors representing documents.

→
INFORMATION

Cost or score

Edge cost, heuristic estimate, Euclidean distance, pattern lookup, or similarity.

→
POLICY

Search strategy

BFS, greedy best-first, A*, IDA*, or a nearest-neighbor index chooses what to inspect.

→
OUTPUT

Result

A path, a sequence of moves, or a ranked list of relevant items.

Route search“How cheaply can I reach that state?”
Rubik’s Cube search“How many moves might remain?”
Embedding search“Which stored vector is most similar?”

Why not put dot product into A*? A heuristic must estimate remaining path cost and ideally be zero at the goal. A raw dot product measures origin-dependent alignment, can be negative, and does not behave like travel cost. It is useful elsewhere—not as a drop-in replacement here.

05 / PUT THE CONTEXT BACK

The representation decides what “near” means.

There is no universally best distance function. A useful score must reflect the legal actions or the semantic structure of the problem being searched.

Why vector databases sometimes treat them as interchangeable

On unit-length vectors, the rankings meet.

||q̂ − x̂||² = 2 − 2(q̂ · x̂)

After normalization, dot product equals cosine similarity. The equation above also makes smaller Euclidean distance correspond to larger dot product. That means all three produce the same ordering—under this specific condition.

Important: mathematical equivalence after normalization does not mean that “dot product is a distance” or that these scores suit every search problem.

Maps and grids

A route has accumulated cost.

A* ranks states with f = g + h. Euclidean or Manhattan distance can provide h when the movement model makes it a safe lower bound.

Rubik’s Cube

Sticker coordinates are misleading.

Distance means moves to a solved configuration. IDA* therefore benefits from pattern databases or cube-specific lower bounds, not geometric distance between stickers.

Embedding retrieval

Usually there is no path at all.

The system ranks stored vectors by closeness or similarity. Cosine and dot product are common because vector direction and magnitude can encode useful signals.
