## August 31, 1994 — Adobe and Aldus Finalize Their Merger

Aldus Corporation and Adobe Systems finalized their merger in 1994. Aldus founder Paul
Brainerd had helped establish desktop publishing with **PageMaker** in 1985. PageMaker
provided page-layout software, but the larger revolution depended on three technologies
working together:

1. **PageMaker** composed text and graphics on a screen.
2. **Adobe PostScript** described a page in a device-independent language.
3. **Apple's LaserWriter** produced professional-looking printed output.

The important lesson is that a platform shift rarely comes from one invention. Desktop
publishing emerged from the combination of an application, a shared representation, and
an affordable output device. Modern AI products have a similar dependency chain: models,
data, compute, interfaces, and deployment infrastructure must work as a system.

Source: [Computer History Museum — August 31](https://www.computerhistory.org/tdih/august/31/)

## September 1, 1994 — Planning a “Virtual Library”

The Library of Congress held the first of several meetings about converting parts of its
collection into digital form. The immediate goals were broader network access and the
preservation of rare or deteriorating materials. Librarians met with technology experts
to discuss priorities, funding, and the practical limits of digitization.

Digitization is more than scanning pages. A usable digital library also needs metadata,
search, storage, durable file formats, access policy, and long-term maintenance. Those
same layers now support web search, research databases, and retrieval-augmented generation
(RAG) systems.

Source: [Computer History Museum — September 1](https://www.computerhistory.org/tdih/september/1/)

## September 2, 1997 — IBM Accelerates the RS/6000 SP

In May 1997, IBM's **Deep Blue** defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov. That
September, IBM announced that its parallel RS/6000 SP platform was 58 percent faster after
processor and software improvements. Deep Blue was a specialized chess system built from
massive parallel search, evaluation software, chess expertise, and custom hardware.

Its victory is sometimes described simply as “a computer became smarter than a human at
chess.” The more useful engineering lesson is narrower: a well-defined problem can yield
to the combination of strong algorithms, domain knowledge, and enough computation. Modern
AI progress still depends on this interaction between models, systems software, and
hardware.

Source: [Computer History Museum — September 2](https://www.computerhistory.org/tdih/september/2/)

## September 3, 1982 — Steve Wozniak's US Festival

After taking leave from Apple following a 1981 plane crash, Steve Wozniak organized the
**US Festival** in San Bernardino, California. The Labor Day weekend event combined a
large music festival with a public technology exposition. Air-conditioned exhibition
tents showcased computing and communications while major bands performed nearby in
temperatures above 100°F.

The festival returned in 1983 but reportedly lost more than $20 million across its two
runs. Financially it failed; culturally it captured an optimistic early-1980s belief that
personal technology, music, and community could shape a new kind of public event.

Source: [Computer History Museum — September 3](https://www.computerhistory.org/tdih/september/3/)

## September 4, 1927 — John McCarthy Is Born

John McCarthy was born in Boston in 1927. He later coined the term **artificial
intelligence**, organized the Dartmouth workshop that established AI as a field, created
**LISP**, and advanced time-sharing and utility-computing ideas. LISP was designed for
symbolic manipulation and became the dominant language of early AI research.

McCarthy's legacy extends beyond a single AI paradigm. LISP popularized ideas such as
garbage collection, interactive evaluation, recursion, and programs that manipulate
symbolic structures. His time-sharing work also helped move computing away from isolated
batch jobs and toward interactive systems shared by many users.

## September 5, 1980 — The Last IBM STRETCH Is Shut Down

Brigham Young University decommissioned the last operating **IBM 7030 STRETCH** in 1980.
IBM had begun the project in 1955 with an ambitious target: create a machine 100 to 200
times faster than existing computers. The first system was delivered to Los Alamos in
1961, but STRETCH reached roughly 25 to 50 times the performance of its competition. Only
eight systems were built.

Measured against its original target, STRETCH disappointed. Measured by its influence, it
was a landmark. Its architecture explored ideas that became central to later computers:

- **Pipelining:** overlapping different stages of instruction execution
- **Lookahead:** fetching and decoding instructions before they are needed
- **Multiprogramming:** keeping multiple programs in progress
- **Error correction:** detecting and correcting certain hardware faults
- **Computer-aided design:** using an earlier IBM 704 to help design a more complex machine

STRETCH influenced IBM's later 7090 and System/360 families and computer architecture more
broadly. It is a classic example of a project missing its headline objective while still
advancing the state of the art.

Source: [Computer History Museum — September 5](https://www.computerhistory.org/tdih/september/5/)

## September 6, 1997 — The USS *Hopper* Is Commissioned

The US Navy commissioned the guided-missile destroyer USS *Hopper* (DDG-70) in San
Francisco. The ship was named for Rear Admiral **Grace Murray Hopper**, whose career joined
mathematics, naval service, and the development of practical programming languages.

Hopper worked on the Harvard Mark I and Mark II, developed the early A-0 compiler system,
and promoted machine-independent programming. Her later language work strongly influenced
COBOL. Naming a warship after her recognized that software and programming-language
abstractions had become strategically important technologies—not merely supporting tools
for hardware.

Source: [Computer History Museum — September 6](https://www.computerhistory.org/tdih/september/6/)

## Connections Across the Week

These events look unrelated, but several themes connect them:

1. **Systems beat isolated inventions.** PageMaker needed PostScript and the LaserWriter;
   Deep Blue needed algorithms, expertise, parallel software, and hardware.
2. **Abstraction expands access.** PostScript separated page design from printer mechanics;
   Hopper's compiler work separated programs from low-level machine details.
3. **Infrastructure preserves and amplifies knowledge.** Digital libraries turn physical
   collections into searchable, networked resources.
4. **A project's legacy can outlive its original scorecard.** STRETCH and the US Festival
   missed financial or performance goals but influenced later technology and culture.
5. **Today's AI rests on the whole history of computing.** It depends on parallel
   architecture, programming languages, digitized knowledge, and integrated software
   ecosystems—not only neural-network algorithms.
